\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On February 16, 1971, the Secret Service, at Nixon's request, began recording his conversations in the Oval Office and the Cabinet Room. Within sixteen months the system had been extended to his office in the Executive Office Building (EOB), to telephones in three rooms and to Camp David. When the system was shut down on July 18, 1973, the collection held 2,719 hours on 4,042 reels \citep{nixonlib2019findingaid}, which the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) cataloged as 22,723 conversations \citep{nara2024conversations} and released in stages until August 21, 2013 \citep{nixonlib2019findingaid}. It was an unusually complete record of private deliberation in a modern presidency, covering the opening to China, the end of the Vietnam War, the 1972 election and, conversation by conversation, Watergate.

Almost none of it had been transcribed. The Watergate Special Prosecution Force (WSPF) and the House Judiciary Committee transcribed the conversations that the prosecutions and the impeachment inquiry needed \citep{wspf,hjc1974eight}. The State Department's historians printed foreign-policy conversations in \textit{Foreign Relations of the United States} (FRUS) \citep{frus1969}, and scholarly editions published selections \citep{kutler1997abuse,brinkley2014nixontapes,brinkley2015nixontapes1973,prde2014nixon,dean2014defense}. Everything else had only NARA's subject logs, which outline each conversation's topics \citep{nixonlib2010subjectlogs}. A registry of published transcripts in machine-readable form, compiled for this work, covered 426 conversations, or 1.9\% of the catalog.

The digitized tapes have heavy amounts of static and background noise, which make transcription difficult. They were recorded at 15/16 inches per second by microphones hidden in the Oval Office desk, beside its fireplace and in the other rooms, and by taps on the telephones, amid overlapping speakers and room noise \citep{nixonlib2019findingaid}. None of the earlier AI-assisted transcriptions \citep{fine2026easynixon,hoover2026nixon,ferguson2026unlocked} documented adapting a speech recognizer to the tapes. The most widely used recognizer, Whisper, was designed to base each word partly on the words it had already written, and it was documented to fall into repetition loops, skip speech and write text over silence \citep{radford2023whisper,koenecke2024careless,baranski2025hallucination,bain2023whisperx}. Parakeet TDT, a FastConformer (a Transformer with convolution) with a transducer decoder, which emits words as it steps through the audio, was designed instead to predict each word chiefly from the audio \citep{xu2023tdt,nvidia2025parakeetv3}.

Both recognizers were therefore used. A large language model was given their two independent readings and the archive's own records, and it returned edits that could each be audited. Earlier systems voted between recognizers \citep{fiscus1997rover} or rewrote one recognizer's candidate transcripts \citep{chen2023hyporadise}. Instead of sorting voices into anonymous clusters \citep{bredin2023pyannote}, a classifier was trained to name the principal speakers. The contributions of this work were the first publicly documented speech recognizer fine-tuned on the tapes, a Transformer model; a pipeline that corrected two recognizers against the archive; speaker labels for fourteen voices; an evaluation on 165 FRUS transcripts held out from all development, with a corpus-wide measurement of single-recognizer artifacts such as repetition loops; and the open corpus, model and code.
