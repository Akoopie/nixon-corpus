\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On February 16, 1971, the Secret Service, at Nixon's request, began recording his conversations in the Oval Office and the Cabinet Room. Within sixteen months the system had been extended to his office in the Executive Office Building (EOB), to telephones in three rooms and to Camp David. When the system was shut down on July 18, 1973, the collection held 2,719 hours on 4,042 reels \citep{nixonlib2019findingaid}, which the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) cataloged as 22,723 conversations \citep{nara2024conversations} and released in stages until August 21, 2013 \citep{nixonlib2019findingaid}. It is an unusually complete record of private deliberation in a modern presidency, covering the opening to China, the end of the Vietnam War, the 1972 election and, conversation by conversation, Watergate.

Almost none of it has been transcribed by hand. The Watergate Special Prosecution Force (WSPF) and the House Judiciary Committee transcribed the conversations that the prosecutions and the impeachment inquiry needed \citep{wspf,hjc1974eight}. The State Department's historians print foreign-policy conversations in \textit{Foreign Relations of the United States} (FRUS) \citep{frus1969}, and scholarly editions publish selections \citep{kutler1997abuse,brinkley2014nixontapes,brinkley2015nixontapes1973,prde2014nixon,dean2014defense}. Everything else has only NARA's subject logs, which outline each conversation's topics \citep{nixonlib2010subjectlogs}. A registry of published transcripts in machine-readable form, compiled for this work, covers 426 conversations, or 1.9\% of the catalog.

The digitized tapes have heavy amounts of static and background noise, which make transcription difficult. To keep maintenance low, the recorders, Sony 800B machines on extremely thin tape, ran at their slowest speed, 15/16 inch per second, so that one reel held up to six and a half hours; neither the machines nor the microphones were made for recording conversations. The microphones were hidden in the Oval Office desk, beside its fireplace and in the other rooms, and the telephones were tapped, so voices overlapped and competed with room noise \citep{nixonlib2019findingaid}. None of the earlier AI-assisted transcriptions \citep{fine2026easynixon,hoover2026nixon,ferguson2026unlocked} documents adapting a speech recognizer to the tapes. The most widely used recognizer, Whisper, bases each word partly on the words it has already written, and it is known to fall into repetition loops, skip speech and write text over silence \citep{radford2023whisper,koenecke2024careless,baranski2025hallucination,bain2023whisperx}. Parakeet TDT, a FastConformer (a Transformer with convolution) with a transducer decoder, which emits words as it steps through the audio, instead predicts each word chiefly from the audio \citep{xu2023tdt,nvidia2025parakeetv3}.

Here we use both recognizers. A large language model receives their two independent readings together with the archive's own records and returns edits that can each be audited; earlier systems vote between recognizers \citep{fiscus1997rover} or rewrite one recognizer's candidate transcripts \citep{chen2023hyporadise}. Instead of sorting voices into anonymous clusters \citep{bredin2023pyannote}, a classifier names the principal speakers. The contributions of this work are the first publicly documented speech recognizer fine-tuned on the tapes, a Transformer model; a pipeline that corrects two recognizers against the archive; speaker labels for fourteen voices; an evaluation on 165 FRUS transcripts held out from all development, with a corpus-wide measurement of single-recognizer artifacts such as repetition loops; and the open corpus, model and code.
